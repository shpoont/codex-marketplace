# A Simple Method for Processing Unorganized Items

Version: **1.4.0**

Work through a collection one item or group at a time: choose the scope, decide
what to do, act and check, then continue.

Use this shared method directly or specialize it for a domain, purpose or
recurring situation. Specialized methods define suitable handling and evidence;
implementations carry out those choices through people, agents, tools or software.
Both preserve this method's meanings and responsibilities. Specialization and
implementation can be combined; simple work needs no separate specialization
document.

## Terminology

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Item** | A thing you can handle on its own or as part of a group. |
| **Container** | Something that contains other items and may itself be an item. |
| **Collection** | The items eligible for this work, within a defined boundary. |
| **Scope** | The item or group from the collection whose handling you will decide together. |
| **Handling decision** | An authorized choice of what should happen, to which items, under which conditions, and what would count as success. |
| **Action** | A step carried out to gather information or change something. |
| **Action targets** | The items an action is directed at, including items examined or changed. |
| **Results** | The actual effects or findings of an action, including any uncertainty. |
| **Processed** | An item or group whose chosen handling has been achieved and verified. |

## Before you start

Choose the collection, purpose and work limit. Define eligible items and included
container contents, including how far into nested containers the work extends.
A boundary can be a rule; no complete list, map or inspection is needed to start.
Exclude later arrivals unless explicitly included.

**Example:** When organizing computer files, the collection could be all files
already in a folder named Downloads and all its subfolders. This boundary includes
nested contents without listing every file.

Use the existing order or current view as a starting point, or choose a useful
one if neither exists. Follow any order required by instructions, purpose,
established policy or dependencies. By default, work from eligible items you
have discovered; make incomplete discovery clear.

When precise ordering matters, resolve relevant ties and selection across included
levels. A required whole-collection order needs adequate evidence, such as a
reliable sorted view. The newest item found may not be globally newest.

Throughout the work, weigh expected benefit and reuse in later work against time
and resources, considering what is already known and the consequences of error.
Reassess when the approach becomes substantially harder or more costly than
expected. Item count alone does not determine effort.

## Choose the scope

You can start with one eligible unprocessed item and expand when useful, or
identify a candidate group containing unprocessed items from an available
overview, such as a list or grouped view. Choose the approach according to the
information available and whether obtaining more would be worth the effort. You
may switch approaches as work develops.

One item is enough. Follow required selection order. Skip blocked items where
required selection order permits, keeping them tracked.

### Refine the scope

You may expand or narrow the scope, including a container, selected contents or
both; it may cover the whole collection. Members need not be adjacent. Follow
required order for selecting items and carrying out actions.

When useful, find candidates by browsing, searching, comparing, following
references or asking for context. Confirm membership from the purpose,
evidence and exceptions. Similarity alone is insufficient, though reliable search
evidence may suffice. Keep uncertain membership pending. Material outside the
collection can inform the decision, but changing it needs separate authority.

Prefer **one appropriate shared handling action** as you refine the scope. Let it
guide grouping provisionally, without distorting the evidence. One item may need
several actions; supporting searches and checks do not count against this
preference.

Keep items together when joint consideration or a shared result matters, even if
several actions are needed. Split only when that helps while preserving
dependencies and without adding disproportionate work.

You may gather evidence for membership or handling when useful; existing evidence
may suffice.

**Example:** Continuing the folder example, search may identify three related
files in Downloads that could be archived together. Information in other files
outside the collection may clarify their relationship. A costly comparison of
file versions may be worthwhile if you are considering deleting a possibly
unique version instead, or if its findings will help with later items.

## Decide what to do

Make or obtain an authorized **handling decision** that serves the purpose and
answers:

- What should happen, and to which targets?
- What conditions must hold?
- What results would satisfy the decision?

Existing authorized policy may supply the decision. When handling has not already
been decided, choose within your authority or obtain the choice from the
responsible decision-maker. Authority to carry out work does not by itself
include authority to choose its handling. Resolve material uncertainty about
who may choose and within what limits before acting on the decision.

A separate view of the scope's items and context is optional.

Recommendations are optional. When useful, prefer one appropriate recommended
handling action, showing known exceptions and necessary combinations from the
outset. Several recommended or final actions may be warranted, including for
different or overlapping targets within one scope.

**Example:** For an email collection, a scope of 20 booking emails may be recommended
for archiving, but the final decision may delete 15 and archive five. That change
does not require splitting or restarting.

## Act and check

Keep decisions, results and unfinished work recoverable.
Unfinished items must remain accessible even after an action moves them outside
the original view.

### Act on the decision

Specify each action and its targets, including effects on container contents.
Targets can cover all or part of the scope and overlap between actions.
A scope may be handled in parts, preserving required order and dependencies.

When approval is required, make the actual final actions, targets, effects and
conditions clear to the decision-maker and obtain approval before execution,
individually or as a set. A clear instruction from an authorized decision-maker,
including a revised instruction, can supply approval when the targets, material
effects and conditions are already understood. Clarify material uncertainty about
the handling or authority before execution; revision alone does not require
another confirmation.

Act within your authority while the decision's conditions hold.

### When arranging follow-up

If arranging follow-up is the chosen handling, specify the next action,
responsibility, accessible information and how the work will reliably be picked
up or reconsidered.
Confirm that the process responsible for the follow-up accepts this work and
preserves the information and conditions needed to continue. An existing way to
accept the work can suffice; a recorded note alone does not complete processing.

### Check completion

Compare the results with the decision; revisit the scope or decision on material
change.

Mark an item **processed** only when evidence verifies that its chosen handling
was achieved. A scope or group is processed only when this covers every member.
Members left out of the decision or with unfinished handling remain unprocessed.
A checked summary or deliberate non-action can satisfy a decision. Keep handling
that depends on a shared result unfinished until that result is confirmed.

Collective evidence, including a verified container action, can suffice without
enumerating or inspecting each member. Container contents count only when their
chosen handling is fulfilled and checked; one action can suffice for all included
contents. Handling the container does not establish individual inspection.

**Example:** In the email example above, verifying the 15 deletions leaves five
emails unprocessed and the scope of 20 unfinished until their archiving is
verified.

### Handle changes and interruptions

Reconsider affected work, including processed items, when new information calls
earlier handling into question. Retain results that remain valid.

Retry when safe; resolve uncertainty about the previous attempt when needed.
Reuse confirmed successes that still apply. If blocked, proceed only with
independent work or an authorized next step.

## Continue or stop

Return to choosing the next scope. An empty current view does not establish
completion: unless evidence already confirms it, continue finding eligible items
within the defined boundary while the work limit allows and authorized work can
proceed. Stop when all included items are processed, the work limit is reached or
no authorized work can proceed.

Report the boundary, coverage, processed items and unfinished or shared obligations.
Claim that the purpose was achieved only when adequate evidence supports the
required results.
Claim zero unprocessed for the whole collection only when evidence covers every
included member and confirms their handling. Processed items may remain present;
items outside the boundary or downstream follow-up may remain unfinished.
